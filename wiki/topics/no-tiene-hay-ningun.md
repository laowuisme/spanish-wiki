## Rule

Spanish uses **double negation** with *ningún/ninguna*. When *ningún* follows the verb, *no* must still appear before it:

> *No tiene ningún problema.* — He has no problem. / He doesn't have any problem.
> *No hay ningún café cerca.* — There's no café nearby. / There isn't any café nearby.

The English equivalent often drops the double negative ("doesn't have **any**"), but Spanish requires both *no* and *ningún*.

## Common Patterns

| Pattern | Example | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| no + tener + ningún/ninguna + noun | *No tengo ninguna idea.* | I have no idea. |
| no + hay + ningún/ninguna + noun | *No hay ningún problema.* | There's no problem. |
| no + verb + ningún/ninguna + noun | *No veo ninguna diferencia.* | I see no difference. |

**Gender agreement:** *ningún* (masc.) / *ninguna* (fem.) — must agree with the noun it modifies.
- *ningún* drops the -o before a masculine noun (like *un* → *ningún* libro)
- *ninguna* keeps the full form before feminine nouns (*ninguna* idea)

**Singular only:** *ningún/ninguna* is almost always singular in Spanish, even when English would use "any" with a plural noun.
